Introduction to Film Scoring

A score decides mood and timing for the image, a second layer of storytelling rather than decoration added afterwards.

Related

Incidental MusicProgram MusicRomantic

Film music is often treated as decoration. A score that works decides how the audience should feel in a scene, and when to worry.

The problem

The image already shows the characters and the action; music that repeats them wastes space. A director needs what the picture cannot say: a sense of time, a premonition, a position the character never voices.

Music and picture

Three basic relations: synchronization, counterpoint and contrast. Synchronization lands the music on the action; counterpoint lets the music say something else; contrast deliberately sets light or lyrical material against violence. Incidental music is the close relative: Grieg's scene music for Peer Gynt and Mendelssohn's music for A Midsummer Night's Dream arrange material scene by scene, and that practice passed into cinema.

Practice and judgement

Read the script, mark turns, reveals and first entrances, then decide which stretches stay silent. Judge by what the image loses when the music is removed. If nothing is lost, the cue is filler.

What to listen for

Hear the move from morning mood to the trolls' hall in Peer Gynt, and how the Egmont overture forecasts the ending through key and scoring. The demo gives a rising three-note horn motive that falls back to the bass, able to sound like a call or a threat.

Common misunderstandings

More music is not better, and silence is part of the score. A score is not a song; lyrics fix meaning and usually belong at the end. Music cannot rescue a bad cut, only amplify what already works.
