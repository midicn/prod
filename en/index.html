Audio & Music Production

The craft of audio and music production — acoustics and listening, recording, mixing and mastering, sound design, game and film audio, plus DAWs, MIDI, arrangement and delivery.

Arranging

  • Arranging Versus CompositionComposition fixes the skeleton, arrangement chooses the vehicle. Know which edits change identity.
  • Bass Line DesignA bass line shapes harmony and rhythmic weight, not just a list of roots.
  • Contrapuntal ArrangingEach line stands alone, and together they still make audible harmony.
  • Designing the Chorus LiftA chorus lifts through register, density and low end at once, not through volume alone.
  • Dynamic ContrastContrast needs a reference. Build the drop with orchestration first, then move faders.
  • Electronic ArrangingElectronic arranging sets the low end and skeleton first, then shapes sections by muting and filtering.
  • Emotional ArcAn emotional arc is built from tension and release, not one long crescendo.
  • Frequency AllocationDecide who owns low, mid and high before anything gets crowded.
  • Groove and Rhythmic CounterpointGroove comes from rhythmic layers complementing each other, not from one busy part.
  • Harmonic BedGive the melody a stable background without competing for its place.
  • Hook DesignA hook must be short, repeatable and transformable, and needs three appearances to stick.
  • Intro, Transition and OutroAn intro introduces one element, a transition changes one parameter, an outro resolves once.
  • Melodic FillsShort figures in the gaps join phrases instead of filling every hole.
  • Part AssignmentWho takes the melody, who fills the inner parts, who holds the bass.
  • Percussion ArrangementSet the kick and snare dialogue first, then decide where the gaps go.
  • Register PlanningWhich octave carries the theme, and how deep the bass reaches, set the weight.
  • Rhythmic DensityHow many events fire per bar decides whether a section relaxes or pushes.
  • Space and BreathSpace gives a phrase a landing point and gives the next entry a reason to arrive.
  • String AdaptationAdapting for strings means reallocating register and attack, not copying every note.
  • Texture LayersSplit simultaneous sound into countable layers, then decide what sits in front.

Orchestration

  • Doubling and ThickeningDoubling thickens a line and can also blur it; register and numbers decide which.
  • Ensemble Size and VenuePlayers and hall reverberation decide which parts can actually be heard.
  • Introduction to OrchestrationOrchestration decides who plays what, where, and how those choices become real sound.
  • Orchestral SeatingSeating decides how sound stacks and who hears whom; strings on the left is not arbitrary.
  • Principles of Instrumental CombinationCombining instruments is not addition, but making loudness, colour and register give way.
  • Register OverlapWhen two instruments share a register, who yields and by how much decides clarity.
  • Scoring for PercussionPercussion scoring fixes timing, weight and space, rather than merely adding volume.
  • Section BalanceOrchestral balance is not about volume but about register, density and how timbres share the spectrum.
  • Solo and TuttiA solo builds focus through contrast; a tutti builds weight through unanimity.
  • The Brass ChoirBrass writing turns on breath, the harmonic series and the precision of unison.
  • The Woodwind ChoirWoodwind scoring works through each instrument's distinct attack and comfortable register.
  • Timbre Blending and MaskingWhen two instruments sound together they either fuse into one colour or mask each other.
  • Timbre as DramaChange the instrument under a theme and the character, the situation, changes with it.
  • Writing for the String SectionString parts need voicing, bowing and dynamics, not a single sustained chord.

Recording

  • Bit Depth and Noise FloorBit depth sets the quantisation noise floor, and therefore how quiet a soft passage can stay.
  • Choosing a Sample RateSample rate sets the highest frequency you can capture, and the file size and workflow that follow.
  • Clocking and SyncDigital devices that keep their own time will drift apart. One master clock, everyone else follows.
  • Close and Distant MikingDistance sets the ratio of direct sound to room sound. Close miking gives detail, distant miking gives space.
  • Comping and AlignmentPick the best fragments from several takes and join them into one seamless track.
  • Digital and AnalogueAnalogue adds noise and saturation, digital adds quantisation and clock error. The method differs.
  • How the Room Shapes a RecordingStanding waves and reflections are printed into the recording. Placement and treatment matter more than a new mic.
  • Live RecordingNo second chance, but a single take that keeps its breath and the real room.
  • Microphone TypesCondenser, dynamic and ribbon microphones transduce sound differently. Choose by source and sound pressure.
  • Miking InstrumentsPianos, guitars, drums and voices need different pickup points. A few centimetres can change the tone.
  • Multitrack RecordingMultitrack recording gives every sound its own track. Isolation and headroom matter more than track count.
  • Noise and HumHiss comes from the circuit, hum from the grounding. Separate them before you treat either.
  • OverdubbingOverdubs only work when each pass locks to the first in time, tuning and breath.
  • Phase ProblemsTwo microphones on one source produce time differences that cancel frequencies. Check phase before tone.
  • Polar PatternsA microphone's polar pattern sets what it hears from each direction, and with it bleed and proximity effect.
  • Preamps and GainThe preamp lifts a microphone's tiny voltage to line level. Gain staging sets noise floor and headroom.
  • Recording BackupA take that lives on one drive is not recorded. A backup must restore on its own.
  • Session Prep ChecklistDecide everything that can be decided early, so the people in the room only have to play.
  • Stereo TechniquesXY, ORTF, AB and MS trade width against phase and mono compatibility.
  • The Recording ChainEvery stage from source to file changes the sound. Learn the order before you touch a knob.

Acoustics and Monitoring

  • Absorption and DiffusionAbsorption removes reflected energy; diffusion scatters it. They solve different acoustic problems.
  • Frequency and WavelengthFrequency sets pitch, wavelength sets how sound meets rooms, obstacles and absorbers.
  • Headphones versus MonitorsHeadphones reveal detail; speakers reveal the room. Neither can replace the other.
  • Hearing ProtectionHearing damage does not reverse; manage level and duration together and rest before the ringing starts.
  • Monitor PlacementPlacement sets low-end, imaging and reflections. Move the speakers before reaching for EQ.
  • Monitor Room TreatmentThe room decides what you hear first; absorption and diffusion follow the problem, not the wall area.
  • Nearfield MonitoringClose speakers make the midrange trustworthy; low end needs another system to confirm.
  • Phase and DelayPhase differences and delay cause comb filtering, changing timbre and stereo image.
  • Reference Listening TrainingFamiliar tracks give listening a stable reference, turning taste into comparable judgement.
  • Reverberation Time (RT60)RT60 describes how fast a room decays and is the baseline for judging recording and mixing spaces.
  • Room CalibrationCalibration makes room errors audible, measurable and correctable instead of a matter of guesswork.
  • Room Modes and Standing WavesParallel walls trap low frequencies in standing waves, boosting some notes and cancelling others.
  • Sound Pressure Level and dBThe decibel is a logarithmic ratio; sound pressure level connects voltage, distance and perceived loudness.
  • What Sound IsSound is a pressure wave in a medium; separating source, room and ear is the first step in monitoring.

Mixing

  • Bus ProcessingThe bus glues and controls peaks; problems belonging to one track must go back to that track.
  • De-essingDe-essing removes only the few excessive sibilants; the consonants still have to survive.
  • Frequency Conflict and CarvingConflict is not solved by boosting both sides, but by giving each band a single owner at any moment.
  • Front-to-Back DepthDistance comes from level, spectrum, transients and reverb together, not level alone.
  • Hearing Common Mix MistakesMud, harshness, flatness and hollowness are four symptoms pointing to four different moves.
  • High-Pass in Daily PracticeSet the corner from the lowest useful note of each part instead of cutting every track at 100 Hz.
  • Introduction to MixingMixing decides who is heard and when, not how good each track sounds on its own.
  • Judging When a Mix Is DoneDone does not mean nothing could change; it means the same checklist passes on several systems.
  • Kick and BassOne owns the transient, the other sustain, so low end needs room rather than more level.
  • Level Balance Is Most of the MixFaders decide what sits forward and back, and many tone problems disappear with balance.
  • Modulation Effects in the MixChorus, flanging and phasing widen a sound and soften its transients at the same time.
  • Mono Compatibility CheckIf the lead, kick and bass survive the mono fold, the width is trustworthy.
  • Panning and Stereo WidthWidth comes from differences between parts and a steady centre, not from hard panning.
  • Parallel CompressionKeep the dry transient and add density from a crushed copy, side by side.
  • Sidechain CompressionOne part steps back when another appears, and timing plus amount matter more than the ratio.
  • Space and ReverbReverb does not add wetness to every track; it places instruments at different distances inside one shared space.
  • The Goals of MixingDecide who should be heard first, then let every other part give up the space it does not need.
  • The Midrange Is the BattlefieldIntelligibility, attack and listening fatigue all live in the midrange, so decide who owns it first.
  • The Order of MixingFoundation before hierarchy, balance before processing; a sound order makes every later step easier.
  • The Purpose of Dynamics ControlCompression is not there to make things louder; it lets the contrast between sections hold together.
  • Using Delay in a MixDelay locks to the rhythm before it creates space; its repeats only help when they land in the gaps.
  • Vocal Processing ChainName the problem before adding a stage, because a longer chain blunts consonants.

Mastering

  • Album Sequencing and GapsTrack order and the silence between songs shape an album's pacing and decide how a listener enters each next track.
  • Dynamic Range and PerceptionDynamic range is not a score to maximise; too much compression removes the breath and impact that make a performance feel alive.
  • Loudness Standards and True PeakMeasuring loudness with LUFS and true peak so a master can be loud enough without clipping after encoding.
  • Master Delivery FormatsA master handoff should state format, sample rate, bit depth and true-peak margin so nobody has to guess.
  • Mixing versus MasteringMixing builds the internal balance, while mastering makes the finished record translate to the outside world without changing its intent.
  • Multiband MasteringSplitting the spectrum so only the problem band is controlled, while the rest of the master keeps its tone and impact.
  • Platform Loudness TargetsStreaming, broadcast and club playback normalise or expect different levels, so a master's loudness should follow its release context.
  • Stereo EnhancementWidening and deepening a master through careful mid-side work while keeping mono compatibility and a solid centre.
  • What Mastering IsThe final pass after the mix, shaping loudness, tone and peaks so a finished track holds up on many playback systems.
  • When to Hire a Mastering EngineerA professional master earns its fee when a record is heading for release, competition, film, games or an album with many tracks.

MIDI

  • Control ChangeControl Change carries continuous gestures such as volume and sustain, one controller number at a time.
  • GM Drum MapOn channel 10 each key number is a percussion instrument, and the program number has no say.
  • GS and XG ExtensionsGS and XG extend GM with banks, drum kits and effects, each with its own reset.
  • General MIDIGM fixes 128 programs, 16 channels and a drum kit on channel 10 so files travel between devices.
  • How MIDI BeganA serial agreement from 1983 that describes pitch, touch and switches in three bytes.
  • MIDI 2.0UMP packs messages into 32-bit words, raising velocity to 16 bits and controllers to 32.
  • MIDI Channels and PortsSixteen channels make one port, and track count is never the same thing as channel count.
  • MIDI Controller TypesCC falls into continuous, switch, bank and parameter families, and the number fixes the meaning.
  • MIDI File FormatSMF stores events in MThd and MTrk chunks, and formats 0 and 1 serve different jobs.
  • MIDI Latency and JitterLatency is a message arriving late; jitter is each arrival being late by a different amount.
  • MIDI Message StructureA message is a status byte plus data bytes, and its frame explains most playback oddities.
  • MIDI and the DAWA DAW turns a MIDI file into tracks, then routes them by port and channel; a track is only a view.
  • MIDI in This LibraryEvery track in the library is an id, a path and a stream of parseable MIDI events.
  • MIDI versus AudioMIDI stores actions and audio stores waveforms, so what can still be changed afterwards differs completely.
  • MPEMPE gives every note its own channel so bend, pressure and timbre follow the note.
  • Note On and Note OffNote On starts a note and Note Off ends it, and the way it ends shapes overlaps and drum decay.
  • Pitch BendPitch Bend sends a 14-bit offset from centre, and an RPN decides how far that offset reaches.
  • Program ChangeProgram Change swaps the timbre on one channel without touching notes, tracks or sounding voices.
  • System ExclusiveSystem Exclusive carries manufacturer settings in 7-bit data, closed by a checksum.
  • Velocity and AftertouchVelocity sits in the second Note On byte, while aftertouch adds pressure held after the key.
  • Virtual Ports and LoopbackA virtual port is a MIDI cable inside the system, and a loopback returns an output to an input.

Sequencing and Clock

  • ArpeggiatorTurns a held chord into a sequence of single notes fired one after another.
  • Clock Source and Master-SlaveSeveral devices can share one time base, but only one of them may be the master clock.
  • Loops and ClipsA loop decides which range repeats, while a clip decides what that range contains.
  • Microtiming and GrooveNotes need not sit on exact grid lines; a few milliseconds of push and pull decide how a groove feels.
  • Piano RollNotes become horizontal bars, so pitch, length and velocity share one editable grid.
  • QuantisePulls performed attacks onto a grid, with strength deciding how far each note moves.
  • Sample-Accurate ClockAudio events land on the sample grid, far more precisely than any MIDI clock.
  • Step SequencerA grid of equal cells where each cell answers one question, whether that step fires.
  • Swing QuantisePushes every second eighth later so an even grid takes on a triplet feel.
  • Tempo MappingTempo is never a straight line; a tempo map draws a performance's breathing onto the timeline.
  • Timecode, LTC and MTCFrame addresses align music to picture, with LTC travelling as audio and MTC as MIDI.
  • Velocity and HumanisingVelocity and tiny timing offsets give a programmed part its breath back.

Synthesis

  • Additive SynthesisStack sine waves one by one and write the harmonic structure directly.
  • FM SynthesisOne oscillator modulates another to make bells, electric pianos and metal.
  • Filters and ResonanceCutoff sets brightness; resonance decides which band around the cutoff speaks first.
  • Formant SynthesisReplace the filter with a few resonant peaks and a synth can sing vowels.
  • Granular SynthesisCut sound into millisecond grains and rearrange them into a cloud.
  • Hybrid SynthesisTake half a sample and half a synth, keeping the realism while gaining the ability to morph.
  • Introduction to SynthesisBefore choosing a method, learn how oscillators, filters and envelopes turn electricity into a playable timbre.
  • Layering and SplitsStack several voices in one range and switch timbre in another; the keyboard becomes a map.
  • Low-Frequency OscillatorA slow oscillator makes no sound of its own, yet keeps the timbre in motion.
  • Mono and PolyOne voice at a time or many at once; the choice changes both playing and arranging.
  • Noise SourcesNoise has no fundamental, yet it supplies drums, breath and texture.
  • Oscillators and WaveformsA waveform is a harmonic recipe that sets the base colour and where filtering begins.
  • Phase DistortionKeep the waveform and bend its phase; a sine gains the edge of a sawtooth.
  • Physical ModellingCalculate the resonance of strings and tubes in real time from physical rules.
  • Sample Playback SynthesisRecord one real note, then stretch, loop and pitch it across the keyboard.
  • Spectral SynthesisSplit sound into a frequency-time map, then rebuild it cell by cell.
  • Subtractive SynthesisStart with a harmonically rich waveform and carve the tone with a filter and envelope.
  • The ADSR EnvelopeFour stages shape the attack, the sustain and the tail of every note.
  • The Modulation MatrixRoute LFOs, envelopes and velocity to any destination, and a static patch starts to move.
  • Wavetable SynthesisA table of single-cycle waves lets the timbre slide while you play.

Sampling

  • Anti-AliasingFiltering above the ceiling before sampling stops high frequencies folding back as false tones.
  • Bit Depth and Quantization NoiseBit depth decides whether the quietest detail survives, and quantization noise is the price.
  • Ethics of Field RecordingBefore recording someone, settle the use, the credit and the chance to withdraw.
  • Granular SamplingChop a sample into millisecond grains and reassemble them to stretch, freeze or cloud the sound.
  • Key and Velocity ZonesKey and velocity zones cut performance into cells, and the sound must join across each border.
  • Multisampling and Velocity LayersSeparate samples per register and velocity keep a keyboard from sounding like a pitch shifter.
  • Round Robin and Articulation SwitchingRepeating one sample exposes the machine; round robin and articulation switching make it sound played.
  • Sample Loops and CrossfadesLoop points keep a sustained note alive; crossfades decide whether the seam is audible.
  • Sample Rate and NyquistSample rate sets the highest frequency a system can capture; Nyquist gives that ceiling.
  • Sampler ArchitectureA sampler is a pool, a map, voices and envelopes working together, not a play button.
  • Sampling Copyright and ClearanceSampling needs two clearances, composition and recording; a file licence is not a work licence.
  • Time Stretching and Pitch ShiftingStretching changes duration, shifting changes pitch, and both leave traces on the material.

Audio Engineering

  • Analog-to-Digital and Digital-to-Analog ConversionSampling and reconstruction are the two gates that decide what digital audio keeps.
  • Audio File FormatsUncompressed, lossless and lossy formats trade file size against compatibility and fidelity.
  • Audio InterfaceThe interface sets how many channels you get, how low latency goes and how stable conversion is; don't forget preamp gain.
  • Audio MetadataA wrong title, composer or work number breaks search and archiving immediately.
  • Audio RestorationNoise reduction, hum removal and click repair all cost something; the harder you clean, the more of the original goes.

  • Fixed Point and Floating PointFixed point has a hard ceiling, floating point has room to spare, and that shapes every mixing decision.
  • Headroom and ClippingDigital full scale is a hard wall; headroom is what lets transients and later processing fit.
  • How Lossy Compression WorksDiscarding masked content saves space, and the cost hides in transients and high frequencies.
  • Latency BudgetInput, processing and output buffers add up to a round-trip delay that decides whether playing feels immediate.
  • Loudness MeteringPeak meters stop clipping and loudness meters track perception; LUFS and true peak each watch one end.
  • Plug-in FormatsVST3, AU, AAX and CLAP decide whether a project opens on another machine.
  • ReplayGainMeasured gain tags even out playback so shuffled tracks no longer jump in level.
  • Sample-Rate ConversionConverting between 44.1 and 48 kHz is not a renumbering exercise; the filter decides how much detail survives.
  • The Loudness WarPushing the master to the ceiling buys loudness at the cost of dynamics and impact.
  • Word Clock and JitterDigital boxes share one clock, and jitter nudges sample points in time until the top end turns harsh.
