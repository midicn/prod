Designing the Chorus Lift

A chorus lifts through register, density and low end at once, not through volume alone.

1 min read25 characters条目ArrangingUpdated 2026-10-28

arrangingchorustexture

Related

RegisterDoublingVoicingVoice

A chorus does not lift because it is louder, but because several parameters change at once. The verse keeps its register low and its layers few, leaving room to rise. If both sections share density and bass, a fader move sounds flat after two listens.

The problem

A chorus that relies on loudness makes the verse fill up and the contrast disappear. The lift must be prepared earlier.

Texture or frequency band

Four changes do most of the work. The melody rises by a third to a fifth, the bass moves from roots to a sustained octave, the drums open from closed to open hats, and the harmonic rhythm slows. Keep the midrange clear for the voice and move pads wider or higher.

Approach

Write the lowest and highest note of the chorus's first line and confirm they sit above the verse. End the verse on a suspension and land the first chorus beat on the tonic. Add only bass and drums at the entry, then bring strings or harmony in the second line. If it still does not lift, remove a verse layer rather than adding one.

What to listen for

Hear the melody change register between verse and refrain in a hymn. Hear strings and woodwind enter later in a game boss loop. Notice whether the bass turns from short notes into a sustained line.

Common misunderstandings

A chorus need not be faster, and many are broader and slower. It need not be higher either, since some lifts come from bass weight and harmonic density.
